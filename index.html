DiabEvidence

Answers only when
the literature does.

People with type 2 diabetes look for answers about food, blood sugar, weight, and medication, and a chatbot that answers confidently without evidence is worse than none. DiabEvidence answers only from published research and is built to decline when the evidence isn't there. Diabetes is the test bed; the real contribution is a method for deciding when a system like this should answer, which carries over to any clinical field by swapping the library.

Topical similarity is not evidence

A passage about the same drug or nutrient doesn't count unless it actually states the claim. One core claim per question decides the verdict.

A scope charter, not code

The scope rules live in one editable file the judge reads at startup, so changing what's in scope never needs a code change.

Safety banners

Flags when evidence comes from a different population, when a comparison is stitched from separate studies, and when someone asks for a personal dose.

Fails closed

On any error, it refuses rather than answers. A broken judge can never let an unchecked answer through.

Two evidence tiers

Research questions search the study abstracts; definition questions search 35 ADA and NIDDK patient-education passages, which can never support a research claim.

Nothing hidden

Direct Anthropic and Pinecone calls with no LangChain. The page streams the answer with the evidence and the judges' reasoning beside it.

Research log

How the method evolved.

  1. The lesson that shaped everything

    Similarity measures relatedness, not answerability.

    After scaling the library from 264 to 5,000 abstracts, a diabetes-adjacent question such as laser treatment for retinopathy scored as high as a genuinely in-scope one. No cutoff could separate them.

    Lower similarity → higher similarity
    Unrelated
    In scope
    Adjacent
    Illustration of the finding, not measured data.
  2. First fix

    One yes/no judge mixed up two questions.

    An LLM answerability judge beat the cutoff, but it collapsed "is this in our subject area?" and "did we find enough evidence?" into one verdict. Valid questions with thin results were told they were out of scope, and passages that were merely on the same topic were accepted: of 13 answered in-scope questions, only 7 were fully supported.

  3. The redesign

    A two-stage judge.

    The scope judge sees only the question and the scope charter, so a good or bad search can't bias whether a question counts. The evidence judge sees the passages without their similarity scores, audits each claim, and checks that the study populations match.

    Stage AIs the question in scope?
    →
    Stage BDoes the evidence state each claim?
  4. What I learned

    Scope is decided by the outcome, not the intervention.

    The first two-stage run sent 7 of 24 in-scope questions to "adjacent" because they mentioned exercise, insulin, or medication. "Do GLP-1 drugs cause loss of lean body mass?" was filed as medication, though lean mass is body composition. I read the judge's reason for every refusal and rewrote the rules: name the outcome first, map it to an area, and never let a word like "insulin" decide on its own.

What still doesn't work

  • Partial-evidence answers overreach. The safety-relevant failure, and the next thing to fix.
  • One scope blind spot: medication questions whose outcome isn't blood sugar, like metformin and vitamin B12.
  • The evidence judge doesn't always apply the population rule.
  • Library gaps: the dawn phenomenon, grapefruit with diabetes drugs, and nuts have no direct evidence in the 5,000 abstracts.

Limitations, stated up front

  • Small samples with wide intervals; most differences between designs are not statistically significant.
  • Development numbers are optimistic: prompts were revised after studying failures on those same questions.
  • The held-out set is semi-independent: it was drafted with the AI assistant used for implementation.
  • Faithfulness is graded by a model; human grading of every answer is still to do.

I designed DiabEvidence and made its key decisions: the scope, the two-stage judge, the routing table, the outcome-based scope rules and revised prompts, the evaluation protocol, and the interpretation of results. I used Claude Code to implement much of the code from my specifications and to run the evaluations. Models: Claude Haiku 4.5 for the judges, Claude Sonnet 5 for answers and grading. A research prototype, not medical advice.

Research Agenda

Three questions I want to answer.

How can large language models be trusted when people rely on them for health information?

Knowing when not to answer.

Answerability and abstention in medical retrieval-augmented generation. In my own system, similarity thresholds could not separate real questions from near-miss ones, so I study better ways for a model to decide when the evidence is enough, and to say so when it is not.

Faithful, evidence-grounded answers.

Checking every claim against the retrieved evidence, and building evaluations that measure groundedness honestly.

Trustworthy health AI for real users.

Robustness under shift, how people read uncertainty and refusals, and moving from diabetes to ADHD, Alzheimer's, cancer, and ACEs.

Built for diabetes. Designed for any disease.

Swap the research corpus and the patient groups, and the same pipeline answers questions about other major diseases, with the same citations and the same refusal to guess.
